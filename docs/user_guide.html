User guide

Indian Stock Data Hub

A free, open-source research desk for Indian listed companies. Enter a few ticker symbols and get each company's key numbers, quarterly trends, latest filings, recent news and a plain-English analysis, then export it all to Excel and Word. The optional AI runs entirely on your own computer.

Chapter 1

What the app does

You type in company tickers; the app collects public information about each one, organises it, and optionally asks a local AI model to summarise it.

1 · CollectRatios, quarterly results, pros and cons from screener.in
→
2 · DownloadThe latest filings: concall transcripts, presentations, annual reports, results
→
3 · ExtractEvery number in those PDFs, with its page and the sentence it came from
→
4 · NewsRecent headlines from six news sources
→
5 · SummariseYour model writes a verdict, an analysis and a news digest
→
6 · KeepEverything is saved locally, searchable, and compared with last time

What you get for each company

  • Key ratios: market cap, price, P/E, ROE, ROCE and more
  • 13 quarters of results, with trend charts
  • Strengths and risks
  • An AI verdict (Positive, Neutral or Cautious) with a short analysis
  • Recent news with an AI digest and sentiment
  • The downloaded filings, and every figure found inside them
  • A detailed table lining up every metric against every source that reports it
  • A list of what has changed since the last time you looked
  • What management promised on the earnings call, and whether it happened
  • A formatted Excel workbook and a Word report

What it isn't

  • Not investment advice. The AI can be wrong. Use it as a starting point for your own research.
  • Not real-time. Prices and ratios are as shown on screener.in when you run the analysis.
  • Not a trading tool. It reads public information; it never connects to a broker.

The AI part is optional. Without it you still get all the data, charts, news headlines, the detailed tables, the archive search and the reports.

Chapter 2

Before you start

Here's what your computer needs. Setting everything up takes 15 to 30 minutes the first time, mostly waiting for downloads.

You needDetails
A computerWindows 10 or 11, macOS, or Linux
Python 3.10 or newerThe programming language the app is written in. Free from python.org/downloads. Chapter 3 shows how to check whether you already have it.
Internet connectionTo fetch company data, filings and news. The AI itself works offline.
Disk spaceAbout 500 MB for the app and its libraries, plus room for filings (roughly 10 to 40 MB per company). The AI model is extra: 0.4 to 8 GB.
Memory (RAM)4 GB is enough without AI. For the recommended AI model, 16 GB or more.
A web browserChrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari. The app opens in your browser but runs on your computer.
New to the terminal? Some steps ask you to type commands. On Windows, open PowerShell (search for it in the Start menu). On macOS, open Terminal (press ⌘ Space and type "Terminal"). Type or paste each command, then press Enter. Lines starting with # in this guide are comments; you don't type them.
Chapter 3

Install the app

Five steps, done once. After that, starting the app is a single command (step 5).

1

Check for Python, install it if needed

Windows

python --version
If you see an error or a version below 3.10, download Python from python.org. On the first installer screen, tick "Add python.exe to PATH". Then close and reopen PowerShell.

macOS / Linux

python3 --version
If it's missing or older than 3.10, install it from python.org (macOS), with Homebrew (brew install python), or with your Linux package manager.
2

Download the app

Easiest: on the project's GitHub page, click the green Code button, then Download ZIP. Unzip it somewhere easy to find, such as your Documents folder.

With git (if you have it):

git clone https://github.com/tapariapurv/indian-stock-data-hub.git

Then move into the project folder. Replace the path with wherever you put it:

cd Documents/indian-stock-data-hub
3

Create a private Python environment

This keeps the app's libraries separate from anything else on your computer. You do this once, inside the project folder.

Windows

python -m venv .venv
.venv\Scripts\activate

macOS / Linux

python3 -m venv .venv
source .venv/bin/activate

Your prompt now starts with (.venv). That means the environment is active.

Windows says "running scripts is disabled"? Skip the activate line and put .venv\Scripts\ in front of the commands in steps 4 and 5, for example .venv\Scripts\python -m pip install -r requirements.txt.
4

Install the app's libraries

python -m pip install -r requirements.txt

This downloads Streamlit (the app framework), the PDF reader, and the Excel and Word writers. It takes a minute or two.

5

Start the app

python -m streamlit run app.py

Your browser opens at http://localhost:8501. If it doesn't, open that address yourself. The first time, Streamlit may ask for an email address; just press Enter to skip.

To stop the app, click the terminal window and press Ctrl C. To start it again another day, open a terminal in the project folder, activate the environment (step 3's second line), and run step 5.

Chapter 4

Choose a model

The app works without any model at all. If you want the verdicts, the written analysis, the news digests, the transcript reading and the archive answers, you need to point it at one — and there are four quite different ways to do that. Pick whichever suits your machine, your budget and your privacy needs. You can change your mind at any time from Settings → Models & keys; nothing else in the app changes.

RouteGood whenCostYour data
A · OllamaYou want the simplest local setup. This is the default.FreeNever leaves the machine
B · A local appYou already use LM Studio, Jan, llama.cpp or similar.FreeNever leaves the machine
C · A hosted APIYou want the strongest models and don't mind paying.You pay per tokenSent to that provider
D · Your own serverYou have a GPU box, a work server or a rented instance.Whatever it costs youStays on that server
E · No modelYou only want the data, tables, charts and reports.FreeNothing to send

Route A · Ollama on this computer

1

Install it

Download from ollama.com/download. On Windows and macOS it is an ordinary installer; on Linux run curl -fsSL https://ollama.com/install.sh | sh. Version 0.9 or newer is needed.

2

Check it is running

Open http://localhost:11434 in a browser. It should say Ollama is running. On Windows and macOS it starts with the computer; on Linux you may need ollama serve.

3

Download a model that fits your machine

Browse ollama.com/library and pull one with ollama pull <name>. Any chat model works. The thing that matters is size: a model needs to fit in memory alongside everything else you are running, and a rough guide is that the download size is close to the memory it will want.

Your RAMAim forWhat to expect
8 GBA model of about 1–2 GBBasic Fast, but small models misread financial context — one we tested called a 7.6% return on equity a "strong position". Treat the verdicts with real caution.
16 GBA model of about 4–8 GBThe sweet spot Accurate summaries that quote the right numbers, typically a second or three each.
24 GB or moreA model of 8 GB and upBetter written analysis and noticeably better at reading transcripts. Slower per answer.

Names and versions change constantly, so this guide deliberately doesn't recommend one. Sort the library by popularity and take a recent general-purpose chat model in your size bracket. Avoid anything labelled embedding — the app hides those, because they cannot write.

4

Point the app at it

Go to Settings → Models & keys, choose Ollama (local), and press Test connection. Every model you have pulled appears in the list with its size. Pick one and save. The research sidebar also has a model dropdown, so you can switch between your installed models for a single run without changing the saved setting.

Route B · An app you already run locally

LM Studio, Jan, llama.cpp's server, text-generation-webui and most others can expose an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. Turn that server on in the app's own settings and note the address it prints — commonly something like http://localhost:1234/v1. Then in Settings → Models & keys choose Custom / self-hosted, paste that address, leave the key empty, and press Test connection. The models that app has loaded will appear in the list.

Route C · A hosted API

Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and OpenRouter are supported directly. OpenRouter is worth knowing about: one key gives you access to models from many companies, which is handy if you want to try several without opening several accounts.

ProviderGet a key fromEnvironment variable
Anthropic (Claude)console.anthropic.comANTHROPIC_API_KEY
OpenAIplatform.openai.comOPENAI_API_KEY
Google (Gemini)aistudio.google.comGOOGLE_API_KEY
OpenRouteropenrouter.aiOPENROUTER_API_KEY

Sign up, create an API key, then paste it into Settings → Models & keys and press Test connection; the provider's models load into the dropdown. Two notes on keys. They are saved in settings.json beside the app, readable only by your user account and never committed to git — but if you would rather not store one at all, set the environment variable above instead and leave the box empty, because the app always prefers the variable. And a hosted model means the scraped ratios, headlines and retrieved passages are sent to that company; nothing else is.

Route D · A server somewhere else

A faster machine on your network, a work server, or a GPU instance you rent by the hour. If it runs Ollama, choose Ollama (local) and replace the address with that machine's, for example http://192.168.1.20:11434. If it speaks the OpenAI API, choose Custom / self-hosted and give it the address and a key if it needs one. Either way the app behaves exactly as it does locally.

Remember that the remote machine must be reachable from this one, and that Ollama only listens to other computers if it was started with OLLAMA_HOST=0.0.0.0 on that machine.

Route E · No model at all

Turn off Use a model in Settings, or just never set one up. You keep every ratio, every quarterly table, every extracted figure, the correlated numbers, the news headlines, the archive keyword search, the saved history and both reports. What you lose is the written verdict, the analysis paragraph, the news digest, transcript reading and the archive's ability to reason about what it finds.

Keeping a paid model on a leash

If you choose a hosted API, set your limits before your first big run. Open Settings → Spending and you will find three:

LimitWhat it does
Tokens per runThe one that actually saves you. A fifty-company watchlist against an expensive model can spend a month's budget in a single click; this stops the run instead.
Monthly limitA ceiling in your own currency, counted from the first of the month.
Monthly token limitThe same idea counted in tokens, useful when you are on a free tier with an allowance.

A call that would cross a limit is refused before it is sent, so a limit cannot be passed by accident; the app tells you plainly that it stopped, and everything else in the run carries on as normal. For the money figures to mean anything, enter what your provider charges per million tokens — input and output — next to its key. The app does not ship a price list, because prices change and a stale one would be worse than none. Local models are always free, are recorded as free, and are never blocked by a limit.

For scale: a company review is about 400 tokens, a news digest about 300, and an archive question about 2,800 including the search steps. Answers are cached, so re-running the same company costs nothing at all.

How long does it take? The first run for a company takes 20 to 60 seconds, mostly downloading and reading its PDFs. A progress bar shows which company is being processed. Chapter 6

Reading the results

Results are organised into three tabs: Overview, Financials and Filings.

Overview: one card per company

  • AI verdict (green Positive, amber Neutral, red Cautious) and a 3-sentence analysis. The line under it names the model that wrote it and how many tokens it used.
  • Key ratios as shown on screener.in. See the glossary for what each one means.
  • Strengths and risks: screener.in's automatic pros and cons.
  • Recent news: up to 8 headlines from the last 14 days, a 2-sentence AI digest, and an overall sentiment (Positive, Mixed or Negative). Click a headline to read the article.
  • Figures extracted from filings: click to open a searchable table of every number found in the downloaded PDFs, with its page and the sentence it came from. These are found by pattern matching, so check the context before relying on one.

Financials: trends at a glance

Pick a company at the top. The four cards show the latest quarter's sales, net profit, operating margin and EPS. The small arrow shows the change from the previous quarter; the line underneath shows the last 8 quarters. Below are a chart of 13 quarters and the complete table, which you can download as CSV.

The other tabs

All numbers lines every metric up against every source that reports it, and lets you search all the extracted figures. What changed compares this run with the last saved one. Guidance tracks what management promised on the earnings call. Each has a chapter of its own later in this guide.

Filings: the source documents

For each company: which filings were downloaded (or why one was skipped, for example because it was larger than 25 MB or wasn't a PDF), with links to the originals. Expand All document links to see every document screener.in lists for the company.

Chapter 7

Analysing a whole watchlist

Typing tickers one at a time is fine for three companies. For thirty, hand the app a file.

The file it expects

A CSV or Excel file with a column headed ticker. Anything else in the file is ignored, so your existing watchlist export will usually work as it is. If there is no column called ticker, the app also accepts symbol, scrip, code or NSE code, and failing all of those it reads the first column.

tickernotes
RELIANCEcore holding
TCS
INFYwatching

The sidebar has a Watchlist template (CSV) button that downloads exactly this file, already filled in with three examples. Open it in Excel, replace the rows, save, and upload it.

Running it

Click Upload in the sidebar, choose your file, and press Run analysis. The uploaded list replaces whatever is in the Tickers box for that run. Duplicates are removed and anything that isn't a plausible symbol is skipped, so a stray heading row or a blank line does no harm.

How long it takes

Budget roughly five to fifteen seconds per company on the first run, depending on how many filings you asked for and how fast your model is. The app pauses briefly between companies on purpose — the sites it reads are free and public, and hammering them is both rude and a good way to get blocked. You can shorten or lengthen that pause in Settings → Data & scraping.

Second and later runs are far quicker. A filing that has already been read is never read again, and an analysis of unchanged data comes straight back from the cache at no cost — the token counter will say 0 tokens this run.

Chapter 8

All the numbers, in one place

The summary tells you the story. The All numbers tab shows you every number behind it, with each source of that number lined up beside the others.

What the table shows

One row per metric. Reading across, you get the value screener.in publishes, what the last two quarters reported and the change between them, then how the downloaded filings describe the same metric: how many times it appears, the most recent value, and the lowest, middle and highest of every mention.

ColumnWhat it means
Screener ratioThe figure shown on the company's screener.in page.
Latest / Previous quarterFrom the quarterly results table, in ₹ crore or per cent.
QoQChange from the previous quarter to the latest one.
In filingsHow many times this metric was found across the PDFs.
Filing latestThe first mention in the most recent document.
Filing low / median / highThe spread across every mention.
SourcesWhich kinds of filing the number came from.

Why the spread matters

A wide gap between low and high is a warning, not an error. It usually means the label caught more than one kind of number — a figure in crore on one page and the same metric as a percentage on another, or a table header that lined up with the wrong column. When something looks odd, use the figures table underneath: it shows every extracted number with its page and the exact sentence it came from, so you can check the source in seconds.

Finding a number

Recognised metrics only is on by default, and limits the table to metrics the app could identify. Turn it off to see every label the extractor produced, including the rough ones. Below, the figures table can be filtered by filing type and category, or searched by text — typing margin finds every mention of margin across every downloaded document for that company.

Both tables download as CSV, and the whole thing also arrives as an All numbers sheet in the Excel workbook and a cross-checked table in the Word report.

Chapter 9

What changed since last time

Every analysis is saved. The next time you run the same company, the app tells you what moved.

The What changed tab

After a run, this tab compares each company with the last saved analysis of it and lists the differences: ratios that moved, strengths and risks that appeared or disappeared, filings that are new since you last looked, headlines you haven't seen, and any change in the AI's verdict. The first time you analyse a company there is nothing to compare, so the tab says so; run it again next week and it fills up.

This comparison is plain arithmetic and text matching. No model is involved, deliberately — a "what changed" list that could invent a change would be worse than no list at all.

The History page

The History page in the sidebar holds every saved analysis. Pick a company and a date to reopen exactly what the app showed you then: the verdict, the ratios, the strengths and risks, the news and the full correlated table. Above it, a chart plots any ratio across all your saved runs, so you can watch the P/E or the ROE drift over months without keeping a spreadsheet.

Each saved analysis also shows what changed between it and the run before it, so you can step back through a company's history one analysis at a time.

Getting back to the paper

A saved analysis is only worth having if you can trace it. Under Where this came from, every filing behind that run is listed with the file it was saved as, the full path to it on your disk, the span of pages its figures came from, and a link to the original document on the exchange or company website. In the numbers table, each metric carries a Pages column listing the exact pages that figure appeared on — so checking a number means opening the PDF and turning to the page, not hunting through sixty of them.

Chapter 10

What management promised

Every quarter, management says things on the earnings call: margins will improve, growth will pick up, capex will land at a number. Nobody ever checks. This does.

Reading the transcript

Open the Guidance tab and press Read the latest transcript. The app finds the sentences where management commits to a future outcome and files each one as a metric, a commitment and a deadline. You need a concall transcript downloaded for that company, so tick Concall Transcript in the sidebar before the run.

This is extraction, not judgement, which is exactly what small local models are good at. The app filters the transcript down to the couple of dozen candidate sentences first, so reading a sixty-page transcript costs a few hundred tokens rather than tens of thousands.

Checking it later

Once a later quarter has been reported, press Check against results. Each commitment from an earlier quarter is graded Delivered, Missed or Unclear against the numbers the app has actually scraped — never against anything the model remembers. The Delivered counter at the top is the useful number: it tells you, over time, how often this management does what it says it will.

Treat a single grade as a prompt to go and read the transcript yourself, not as a verdict. Treat a pattern across eight quarters as worth knowing.

Chapter 11

Searching everything you have downloaded

Every filing the app downloads stays on your machine and stays searchable — across companies, with no network and no cost per search.

Asking a real question

When a filing is read, its text is stored in a small database alongside the extracted figures. The Archive page searches all of it at once. Type a question the way you would ask a colleague — what did management say about capex and expansion plans? — and leave Let the model do the searching and answer switched on.

Four things then happen, and the model does the two that need judgement:

StepWhat happens
1 · VocabularyThe model lists the words a filing would really use. Ask about "capex" and it adds capital expenditure, expansion plans, growth strategy, investment plans. This is the step plain keyword search cannot do: the gap between your question and a transcript is wording, not logic.
2 · RetrievalThe database finds the pages matching any of those terms and ranks them by relevance.
3 · JudgementThe model reads the shortlist and throws out the coincidences — the page about share capital when you asked about capital expenditure.
4 · AnswerIt answers from the passages that survive, quoting the figures and citing each passage as [1], [2] after the claim.

On a real Reliance archive, asking about capex returned an answer citing a capital expenditure of about ₹39,000 crore, drawing on both the concall transcript and the annual report — in six seconds, on a laptop, for about 2,800 tokens. The model only ever sees the passages retrieved from your own documents, so the answer is grounded in the filings rather than in whatever it happens to remember about the company.

Every result shows which company, which filing and which page it came from, along with where that PDF sits on your disk, so you can always go and read the original. You can narrow a search to particular companies or filing types, and expand any result to read the full page.

Switch the toggle off — or run without a model at all — and you still get plain keyword search. It just cannot reason about what it finds.

Chapter 12

Excel and Word reports

The Excel workbook and Word report buttons appear above the tabs after a run. Both contain everything from that run.

Excel workbook

  • Overview sheet: summary tiles and one row per company, with verdicts, ratios and quarter-on-quarter changes. Click a ticker to jump to its sheet.
  • One sheet per company: AI analysis, key ratios, strengths and risks, news with links, all quarterly results with a chart, and the filings.
  • Figures sheet: every number extracted from every PDF in one filterable table. Use the arrows in the header row to filter by ticker, category or label.

Totals and changes are live formulas, so they update if you edit the numbers. Sheets print landscape, one page wide.

Word report

  • Cover page with an "At a glance" table of all companies.
  • One page per company: verdict, AI analysis, ratios, strengths and risks, the last 5 quarters, news with clickable links, and the filings.
  • A4 layout with page numbers, ready to print or share as PDF (File → Save As → PDF in Word).

The Word report shows 5 quarters so the table fits the page. The Excel workbook has all of them.

Chapter 14

Privacy and how the AI is used

The AI runs on your computer through Ollama. None of your data is sent to an AI company.

What leaves your computer

Only ordinary web requests for public pages: company data from screener.in, filing PDFs from the stock exchanges and company websites, and headlines from Google News, Bing News, Economic Times, Livemint, Business Standard and Hindu BusinessLine. The app has no accounts, no tracking and no analytics.

What the AI sees

AI taskInputTypical tokens
Verdict and analysisKey ratios, the last two quarters' results, and up to 4 strengths and 4 risks≈ 400
News digest and sentimentUp to 8 headlines (not the full articles)≈ 300
Error explanationThe error message, only when a company fails≈ 100

A token is roughly ¾ of a word. The app keeps usage low: it sends only clean, structured data (the thousands of PDF numbers are too noisy); caps every reply's length; switches off hidden "thinking"; and reuses answers for an hour (30 minutes for news), so re-running never pays twice for the same data. The Overview tab shows exactly how many tokens each run used.

Not investment advice. Verdicts come from a small language model reading scraped data. It can misread numbers or miss context. Always check the underlying figures and filings before making decisions.

Using data sources responsibly

The app fetches public pages the way a browser does, pauses between companies, and keeps downloaded PDFs so they're never fetched twice. Please keep it that way: analyse a handful of companies at a time, and respect each website's terms of use.

Chapter 15

The Settings page

Almost everything about the app can be changed from inside it. Your choices are stored in settings.json next to the app, and nothing here can break it permanently — there is a Reset button at the bottom of the last tab.

Models & keys

Choose your provider, give it an address and a key if it needs one, and pick a model. Test connection tells you immediately whether it works and how many models it can see. Under Generation limits you can set how long each kind of reply may be, in tokens; these are deliberately tight, and they are the difference between a tidy three-sentence answer and a surprising bill.

Spending

Only relevant if you use a paid provider; a local model is free and is never counted. Here you set a limit per run, a monthly limit in your currency and a monthly token limit, and see exactly what has been spent this month — by provider, by model and by what it was spent on, with a daily chart. A call that would cross a limit is refused before it is sent. Chapter 4 explains the three limits and which one matters most.

Data & scraping

The tickers, filing types and timeframe a new run starts with, plus the limits it works under: the largest filing to download, how many pages to scan, how many filings to fetch at once, how long to pause between companies, and how recent news must be. The same tab has switches to turn whole features off — if you never look at news, or never want anything saved, turn it off here and the tabs disappear.

SettingDefault and meaning
Largest filing25 MB. Anything bigger is skipped rather than downloaded.
Pages scanned per PDF150. Most filings are well under this, and a document is only ever scanned once.
Parallel downloads4. Filings fetch at the same time; higher mostly annoys the servers.
Pause between companies1–2 seconds. Politeness towards free public sites.
Headline age / count14 days / 8 headlines.
Result cache60 minutes. Takes effect after a restart; Clear cached results works at once.

Appearance

Accent colour, text size, spacing, chart colours, table height, decimal places, whether metrics show sparklines, and which tabs appear on the research page and in what order. You can also rewrite the subtitle under the app's title. If anything ever looks wrong, switch off Apply my appearance settings and the app falls straight back to its built-in theme.

Storage & history

This is where you decide how long the app remembers things. Each limit can be set to 0, which means keep forever.

WhatDefault
Saved analyses180 days, and at most 40 per company. This is what the History page shows.
Filing PDFs90 days. Deleting these frees the most space; they are downloaded again when needed.
Searchable text365 days. Keep this longer than the PDFs — it is small, and it is what the Archive page reads.

With Apply these limits automatically switched on, the clear-out happens quietly when the app starts. You can also run it on demand, clear the caches, or — behind a confirmation — delete every saved analysis and everything indexed.

Settings that live outside the app

Two things are still set when you start the app rather than inside it. To run on a different port:

python -m streamlit run app.py --server.port 8502

And to reach Ollama on another machine you can either type the address into Settings or use the OLLAMA_HOST variable, which is the same one Ollama itself reads:

Windows (PowerShell)

$env:OLLAMA_HOST = "192.168.1.20:11434"
python -m streamlit run app.py

macOS / Linux

OLLAMA_HOST=192.168.1.20:11434 \
  python -m streamlit run app.py

Chapter 17

Glossary

The terms you'll meet in the app, in plain language.

Ticker / symbol
A company's short code on the stock exchange, e.g. INFY for Infosys.
Crore (Cr)
10 million (1,00,00,000). Indian financial figures are usually in ₹ crore.
Market cap
Share price × number of shares: what the whole company is worth on the market.
P/E (price-to-earnings)
Share price ÷ earnings per share. Higher means investors pay more for each rupee of profit.
Book value
The company's net assets per share, from its accounts.
ROE (return on equity)
Profit as a percentage of shareholders' money. Shows how efficiently that money is used.
ROCE (return on capital employed)
Operating profit as a percentage of all capital used, including debt.
Dividend yield
Yearly dividend as a percentage of the share price.
Face value
The nominal value printed on a share. Rarely matters for analysis.
Sales / revenue
Money earned from the company's business in the period.
Operating profit
Sales minus operating expenses, before interest, depreciation and tax.
OPM (operating profit margin)
Operating profit as a percentage of sales.
Net profit
What's left after all expenses, interest and tax.
EPS (earnings per share)
Net profit ÷ number of shares, in rupees.
QoQ (quarter on quarter)
Change from the previous quarter. Rising sales or profit is shown in green; rising costs are shown in red.
pp (percentage points)
The change between two percentages: a margin going from 20% to 23% is +3 pp.
Concall transcript
The written record of management's earnings call with analysts.
Investor presentation
The slide deck a company publishes alongside its results.
Ollama
Free software that runs AI language models on your own computer.
Model
The AI "brain" Ollama runs. Bigger models are usually more accurate but need more memory.
Token
A chunk of text, roughly ¾ of a word, that AI models read and write.
Verdict
The AI's one-word take (Positive, Neutral or Cautious), based only on the scraped data.
